BUNDLED STYLE
Briefing
A dark style that speaks through spacing and rules.
Structure the information; do not decorate it.
Dark ground / one teal accent / direct claims / no shadows
Core rules of this style
DO — because the room must scan the claim
State the claim directly below the title.
Separate fields with rules, labels, and space.
Lead with concise icon-and-text rows.
Set numbers large and units small.
Use one accent in one or two places.
Whitespace is structure; remove content to keep 5% margins.
DO NOT — because extra signals dilute order
Never show a label without its claim.
Do not rely on cards; use bands, rules, washed panels, and space.
Use neither emoji nor shadows.
Keep titles short and fields open.
Vary each slide’s composition.
Use direct terms, not metaphor.
Do not enclose—separate. Do not pack—open space. Do not decorate—align.
Contrast: 4.5:1 for text; 3:1 at 18pt and above.
One-direction message depth
Every slide has a title and topic sentence, begins with facts, and ends with an implication.
RULE 1 — STATE THE CLAIM
A short title label states no claim, so the reader must supply “so what?”
Put a subject-and-predicate claim below the title; never make a label-only slide.
RULE 2 — ORDER BY DEPTH
Place facts above and interpretation below, so depth increases down the page.
Do not state an implication without visible evidence above to support it.
RULE 3 — COMPRESS
Lead with the conclusion, cut dispensable words, and never repeat a point.
A sentence that wraps because it carries two claims is a signal to split the slide.
Read only the topic sentences in order; they must form one continuous argument.
Emphasis through colour and voice
Write the most important sentence in the most readable colour; keep spoken prompts off the page.

the full text colour. Use muted text only for a qualifying phrase; muting the whole claim makes it disappear.

Direct nouns and unambiguous statements
The slide is a document the audience may revisit, not a transcript of the presenter.
Write about the subject itself.
• One distribution or coordination decision
• Working flow demonstration
• Implementation issues and mitigations
Do not narrate the meeting or use metaphor.
• Today is for finding a direction
• Experience the demonstration
• Hidden traps or silver bullets
• Gravity, foundations, or iron rules
Choose direct words that retain one meaning when read later.
Title grammar standards
A display title is a formal noun phrase derived from the outline claim, not a copied question or conversational sentence.
1 Noun phrase
Use a noun phrase so no question or invitation is printed.
2 Formal vocabulary
Use formal terms that name the model, role, or condition.
3 No rhetoric
Remove rhetoric and suspense punctuation from the title.

Separation of printed and spoken language
A hypothesis may be asked aloud, but the slide records the precise subject and current position.
Do not print questions or calls to the room.
“Would this handoff work here?” / “How much could this role own?” / “Any reactions?”
Print a noun phrase or short assertion; place the question in speaker notes.
Printed: expected operating model
Printed: ownership after handoff
Notes: “Would this work here?”
Notes: “How much could this role own?”
The printed page works alone; dialogue remains spoken.
Outline claims become title-topic pairs
The orchestrator keeps each outline entry as one sentence claim; the composer splits its display without changing meaning.
STRUCTURE
A 20-minute talk uses 10–16 slides in two to four chapters.
Open with context and close with implication; add a divider and mini map for three chapters.
Keep each outline as one claim; display it as a noun title and topic sentence.
DENSITY AND VISUALS
Spoken decks use 25–45 words and notes; read-alone decks use 60–90 words and larger boxes.
Use direct metrics for numbers, matched halves for comparisons, chevrons for process, and swimlanes for ownership.
Use cycles for recurrence and prose for chronology; avoid decorative networks and empty grids.

PATTERN / TEXT
Sectioned evidence summary
Complete sentences under section headers let the audience follow evidence without a diagram.
Frame the issue
Separate context, evidence, and implication into one sentence each; split a sentence that carries two claims.
Write the evidence
  • Each bullet states a subject and action rather than a list of nouns.
  • Emphasise one word per line; bolding everything removes hierarchy.
  • Use at most two levels; a third level requires another slide.
Qualify precisely
Place a source, exception, or limitation beside the claim in muted text.

PATTERN / PARAGRAPHS
Narrative evidence sequence
When chronology matters, use paragraphs and raise only the turning point.
First state the observed condition: who encountered what constraint, and under which comparable scope.
Then state the measured turning point. This is the central sentence; the preceding condition and following implication exist to make its meaning precise.
Finally state the bounded implication: what changes now and which condition still limits expansion.
MESSAGEA diagram is a means, not a goal; use prose when prose carries the reasoning faster.
