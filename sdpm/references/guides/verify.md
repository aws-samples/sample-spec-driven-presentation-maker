# Verify

A style and a guide carry preferences. They can be replaced: another style wants a
different density, another guide names different forms, and any deck is free to swap both.
What is below cannot be replaced, so check it against the slide you built rather than the
slide you meant to build.

**It is a short list on purpose.** Nothing here is about how much to put on a slide, how
plain or how decorated it should be, or which form is nicer. Those are the style's, and a
style that wants more decoration than another is not wrong. What follows is only the
claims a slide makes that can turn out to be **false** — where the drawing says one thing
and the content says another, or the text says more than its evidence does. `design-rules`
and `visual-forms` say how this deck draws.

## What the drawing asserts

A visual decision can encode something, and when it does it is readable in a second,
before any text is. That is why a wrong one is worse than no drawing at all: the audience
believes the shape and only later reads that the content says something else. Each row
below applies **only when the slide is using that element to encode the thing beside it**.

| Element | When it encodes | It is false when |
|---|---|---|
| An arrow or a connector | a direction or a relationship between two things | it joins things with no relationship, or runs one way where the content runs both, or the other way round |
| An axis, a scale, a tick | positions along it are measured | the positions were chosen to fit the layout, or nothing on the slide says what the scale is |
| Size | a quantity | the sizes are not proportional to it — a doubled value drawn at twice the width *and* twice the height is four times the area |
| Colour | a category | the same colour stands for different categories, or one category changes colour, across the deck |
| Position in a sequence | order, rank or time | the content's order is not the order shown |
| An enclosure or a shared background | these belong together and the others do not | the grouping shown is not the content's grouping |
| An icon | the concept beside it | one icon stands for two different concepts in the same deck |

Where the content encodes none of these, the shape is free: a drawing that is there to be
looked at is a legitimate choice, and the style decides how far to take it.

## Whether the text is true

The deck is read as a claim about the world, and the audience cannot check it. Nothing on
a slide may say more than the evidence behind it says.

- **Numbers come from the evidence, unchanged.** Same value, same unit, same period, same
  basis. A rounded figure says it is rounded. A number nobody can point to does not go on a
  slide.
- **Quotes are verbatim and attributed.** Trimming is allowed and marked; rewording is not.
- **No fact arrives from nowhere.** Every name, date, figure, ranking and causal claim
  traces to the brief's Sources, or to the user's own words in the brief. A plausible fact
  you supplied yourself is the one failure the audience cannot detect and the speaker cannot
  defend.
- **A gap stays a gap.** Where the outline says `[TBD]`, the slide says so too. Filling it
  with something reasonable hides the one thing the author needed to see.
- **Labels say what the thing is.** Axis labels, units, legend entries, column headers and
  footnote markers all resolve. "Other" and "Total" mean the same thing on every slide of
  the deck.
- **Whatever carries the slide's claim is supported by the slide.** In some styles that is
  the headline, in others a lead line under a topic headline — the style decides which. If
  the body and the visual do not establish that claim, either it overstates or the slide is
  missing its evidence. Weaken the claim or add the evidence; do not leave the gap.
- **Hedged in the source stays hedged on the slide.** An estimate, a forecast and a target
  are not results, and a slide that drops the qualifier asserts something the source did not.
