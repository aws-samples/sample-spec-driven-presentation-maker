# Story Line

A deck's slides are a sequence, and the sequence is what persuades. Decide the line the
story takes before writing the outline: it fixes what the first slide is for, what the
last one leaves behind, and which slides are allowed to exist in between.

Work in this order. Who the audience is, then one backbone for the whole deck, then how
many slides there is room for, then the opening. Write the outline, then check it against
the list further down. The vocabulary at the end is for shaping individual slides once the
sequence holds.

## Who you are writing for

The brief names the audience. Assume less of them than the brief implies, in six specific
ways, because each one is a gap between you and them that no amount of effort closes:

- **Knowledge.** You know enough about this to present it. They are hearing it for the
  first time, or heard it once and have forgotten.
- **Foresight.** You know what has been covered and what is coming. They have half
  forgotten the last slide and have no idea what follows.
- **Comprehension.** You understand the content. They are understanding it while it moves,
  and have not fully understood what came before.
- **Attention.** You are presenting. They are receiving, and anything in the room competes
  with you.
- **Motivation.** You have a reason to present. They often have no particular reason to
  listen and are thinking about something else.
- **Condition.** You prepared for this. They are short of sleep, jet-lagged, or worrying
  about their own talk.

So design for the least attentive audience the brief admits, not the most expert one. The
cost of that is small: a deck built for someone who is not concentrating is at worst mildly
slow for someone who is, and that is not a failure. The reverse — a deck built for the
expert — simply does not land.

## Pick one backbone

Each family answers a different question about the audience. Read the condition first and
let it choose — the frameworks named inside a family are variations on the same move, so
the family matters more than which name you pick from it.

### Conclusion-First

**When**: the audience already has the context, or has no time. Executives and
decision-makers expect this; burying the conclusion loses them before you reach it.

**Shape**: lead with the recommendation, then justify it.

**Frameworks**: minto-pyramid (SCQA: situation → complication → question → answer),
SCR (situation → complication → resolution), answer-first / top-down.

### Persuasion Sequence

**When**: the audience does not yet feel the urgency. Offering the answer first would
make it sound like a solution to a problem nobody has.

**Shape**: build the need, then satisfy it. The gap between "this is broken" and "here is
how to fix it" is what creates the momentum.

**Frameworks**: monroe-motivated-sequence (attention → need → satisfaction →
visualization → action), AIDA (attention → interest → desire → action), problem-solution
(problem → amplification → solution → evidence → path).

### Contrast / Vision

**When**: the audience needs to see a future worth moving toward, and the present is not
painful enough on its own to move them.

**Shape**: alternate between current pain and possible gain. The oscillation builds the
desire for change.

**Frameworks**: what-is/what-could-be (Duarte), before-after-bridge, gap-analysis.

### Narrative Arc

**When**: the audience needs to feel the journey rather than hear the conclusion, and
your credibility comes from having been through the struggle.

**Shape**: transformation over time — a situation, a turn, a different situation.

**Frameworks**: hero's-journey (business), failure-lesson-success,
challenge-choice-consequence, kishōtenketsu, fichtean-curve, story-circle.

### Logical Grouping

**When**: the content is broad or many-sided and no single thread runs through it, so the
audience's problem is orientation rather than persuasion.

**Shape**: group by theme, time or impact, and give the audience the map first. Nested
loops keep several threads alive without losing cohesion.

**Frameworks**: by-topic, chronological, highlight-top-N, nested-loops, frame-story.

## How many slides

The brief gives the time. The count follows from it, and it is worth fixing before writing
the outline, because it decides how many messages the deck can carry.

| Talk length | Slides |
|---|---|
| about 10 minutes | time ÷ 1–1.5 min |
| 15–30 minutes | time ÷ 1–2 min |
| 60 minutes or more | time ÷ 1.5–3 min |

Both bounds matter. **No slide gets less than 30 seconds**: below that, an audience that
glances away misses it entirely, and every change of slide costs them some of what they
were holding in mind. But a slide that takes much longer than the band suggests is either
too dense to read — small type, too many figures — or thin and being carried by talk the
slide does not show, which an audience cannot go back to. Notice that the band widens as
the talk lengthens: the same slide deserves more time in a long talk, because the audience
tires.

With the count fixed, decide the proportions before writing anything: so much for getting
in, so much for the substance, so much for what it means and what is next. That split is
not only time — it is how many slides, and therefore how many messages, each part gets.

## The opening

The opening does two things, and both have to work. It makes the audience want the rest,
usually by stating plainly the question the deck answers. And **it carries the deck's point
on its own**: someone who hears only the opening should know what you are saying, even
without the detail that supports it.

When both hold, shorter is better. One or two slides is fine, and a short opening is a sign
the deck knows what it is about. What it must not do is shrink to the point where the
audience cannot see why the question matters.

If the subject genuinely needs a long run-up, do it in two stages: open on a goal that is
easy to agree with, arrive there, and only then open the real subject. An audience does not
know what to pay attention to until the opening ends, so one long opening loses them.

## Check the outline against these, in this order

The order is the order of the constraints: a failure high on the list is not fixed by
anything lower down.

1. **Does it fit the time?** Count the slides, overall and per part, against the band
   above. Running over is worse than useless — it costs the audience's goodwill.
2. **Is what the brief asked for in here?** The brief says what the audience should
   believe or do afterwards. An outline that does not carry that is not finished, however
   good it reads.
3. **Does each slide follow from the one before it?** Each slide has to be understandable
   from **the immediately preceding slide alone** — not from anything earlier, which the
   audience has already let go of. Prefer a sequence that reads as "and so": a slide that
   contradicts what the one before it set up makes the audience's expectation and the
   deck's content diverge, which is the hardest thing to recover from. Where a reversal
   really is the point, signal it on the slide before, so the turn is expected:
   "A is strong, but it has a problem → where A fails" rather than "A is strong → but A
   has a problem".
4. **Is it concrete?** Everywhere, prefer the specific and slightly imprecise over the
   accurate and abstract. Decide at this stage where the examples go; an opening and a
   body made entirely of examples is a good outcome. Being understood matters far more
   than being exact.
5. **Is the difficulty right?** Start where the whole audience can agree or follow, or they
   will not start at all. End somewhere they did not already know, or there was no point.
   But do not stay in unfamiliar territory: after each climb, come back down. Plotted, the
   difficulty of a good deck is a sawtooth, not a ramp.
6. **Is the tension managed?** An audience can take about three slides of difficult or
   serious content in a row. Roughly one slide in three should be one they can follow
   without effort. Controlling the difficulty in 5 does most of this by itself.

## What you left out

Designing this way leaves material unused. Dropping it entirely is often wrong: audiences
differ in what they already know, and the sequence that is best for most of them is not
best for all. There are four things to do with it, in roughly this order of usefulness.

- **Fold it into what comes after the main argument.** Related work, limitations, what is
  next. This is where material that did not fit the line belongs, and it is also where the
  full shape of the subject becomes visible.
- **Name it without covering it.** One slide saying "there is also this, which we are not
  going into". Cheap, keeps the line clean, carries little information. Good for what the
  deck implies anyway, or what matters only to specialists.
- **Hold it for questions.** Harmless, but the audience never sees it, so do not spend
  effort here. Material cut during rehearsal is the natural candidate.
- **Nothing.** Deciding what does not need a home is part of the work.

## Shape the slides inside it

The backbone decides the order. These decide what a single slide does.

### Narrative Technique

**Duarte framework**: sparkline, new-bliss.
S.T.A.R. moment: memorable-dramatization, repeatable-sound-bite, evocative-visual,
shocking-statistic, emotive-storytelling.

**Persuasion mode**: kairos, social-proof, authority-appeal, scarcity.

### Rhetorical Device

**Repetition**: anaphora, epistrophe, anadiplosis, diacope, symploce.

**Structure**: tricolon, climax/auxesis, antithesis, chiasmus.

**Rhythm**: asyndeton, polysyndeton, isocolon.

**Imagery**: hyperbole, litotes, synecdoche, metonymy.

### Slide Technique

**Opening**: cold-open, hook-question, startling-statistic, provocative-statement,
"imagine…" scenario.

**Pacing**: progressive-disclosure, build-slide, pattern-interrupt, pregnant-pause,
callback, bookend, cliffhanger.

**Closing**: circular-close, memorable-last-line, Q&A-bridge.

### Data Storytelling

**Framing**: data-ink-ratio, chartjunk, small-multiples.

**Narrative with data**: lead-with-insight, build-the-case, trend-then-anomaly,
zoom-in-zoom-out.

**Emotional data**: humanize-the-number, anchor-to-familiar, unit-chart/isotype.
